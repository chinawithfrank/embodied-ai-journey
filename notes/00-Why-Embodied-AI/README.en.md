# Why Embodied AI

## Why I learned this

AI capability is growing exponentially. One day, a single sentence will be enough to build any software product, no matter how complex.

Maybe it can't do that yet — but I believe it eventually will. Because the underlying pattern of AI progress is: **if you can define the problem clearly, AI will eventually be able to solve it**. Writing software is, at its core, turning a clearly defined problem into executable logic — which is exactly what AI is best at, and improving at the fastest.

If that holds, the value of pure software keeps getting diluted: the barrier to writing code keeps dropping, and the moat programmers built on "I can implement this" keeps eroding.

So the question becomes — where does a programmer go from here? I set two constraints for myself:

1. **Don't abandon the skills I've already built** — years of engineering ability and systems thinking shouldn't go to waste.
2. **Go toward a market with real headroom** — one that keeps growing, instead of one AI can simply absorb outright.

If pure software isn't it, what about software + hardware? Connecting AI's capability from the virtual world into the physical world — not just generating code or text, but perceiving, deciding, and acting — is a problem that's nowhere close to solved; it's just getting started. And looking around, the only vehicle with real prospects for carrying that is the robot — **Embodied AI**.

That's the starting point of my move from software engineer to embodied intelligence.

## Concept

Embodied AI, simply put, means giving AI a "body":

- **Perceive** — understand the physical world through cameras and sensors.
- **Decide** — reason and plan based on what's perceived.
- **Act** — change the physical world through motors, arms, and mobile bases.

Unlike pure software AI, embodied AI has to face the uncertainty of the real world: noise, latency, physical constraints, irreversible failure — problems that barely exist in the software world but show up daily in robotics. That's exactly why it's far from being "solved with one sentence" — and why it's a genuine, investable, incremental market.

## Risks

This is just my current thesis, and it could well be wrong — worth re-checking as I go:

- AI replacing the value of software might happen slower than I think; "pure software has no value" may be too aggressive a claim.
- The barrier to entry for robot hardware, supply chains, and safety is much higher than software, and the payoff cycle could be longer than expected.
- I don't have a robotics/mechanical/electronics background yet — this whole path is itself a from-zero learning experiment.

Writing these concerns down now is so I can come back later and check whether this thesis actually held up.
